i take great pride in my technical prowess in all things computer-y. it's the one thing i think i am truly exceptional at, and it's something i do both professionally and personally. i have a homelab, i work in IT, i used to be web development and i am always eager to stretch my legs a bit and figure out a new piece of technology. learning to program because i wanted to make mods for garry's mod was a pivotal moment in my life, because it would steer me into a prosperous career and earn me a living when it came time to become a contributing taxpayer. all of this is to say, i am usually of the mind that technology is interesting, that i am eager to play with new technology, and that i can name a lot of times where breakthroughs in tech did good in the world. 

machine learning is a technology i do not take foundational issue with. there's a lot of good things that can be made with it that have historically been out of scope for traditional computing. every time i use my google assistant in my car to navigate to a street mere kilometres from my current location i am forced into an argument with it, and i am reminded of how impractical it was (and still is often!) to communicate with a computer using natural language using the methods of just 5 years ago. machine learning is good at this! there's genuine use cases for it, and it's been mainstream for longer than i think most people realize.

but, this diary post isn't about the general use of machine learning. i'm not talking about the recognition models that is able to be ran in real time on a tiny soc inside of a camera. it's about LLMs ran by entities such as google, microsoft, openai and anthropic, and the people who use them them to make themselves redundant in their own hobbies.

if i was a business owner, i can see why you'd love these things. my boss certainly does. it saves time, they're relatively cheap for the average user (free depending on the use case), and they're impressive. the LLMs we have now are very presentable pieces of technology. they feel engineered to wow the csuiters and middle managers by doing everything they asked simply, quickly and with quirky, friendly language that is crammed full with emojis for some reason. look! the robot did all of the work you could do in 4 hours, and it did it in less than 5 minutes! isn't that cool? this is the fork in the road where either the affected worker has their position made redundant, or if they're slightly more fortunate (but still pretty unfortunate), their job security is not so subtly threatened lest they debase themselves and start using the technology to "optimize" their workflow.

obviously, i have my reservations. i'm not entirely innocent here, i've written a handful of e-mails at work with copilot (again, at the behest of the man who signs my paycheques), i've fiddled around with stable diffusion models, and out of sheer curiosity recently i burnt my entire github copilot tokens for the month, just to see what it would do. i gave it a single prompt (make me an esp32 usb optical drive emulator that reads from an sd card and pretends to be a USB MSC cd drive) and off it went. for the next 15 minutes, it read online articles, parsed thousands of pages in documentation, rarely asked for any intervention except for the dozens of search requests i had to manually approve (presumably you're meant to get sick of this and inevitably tell it to go buck wild and take the leash off, naively hoping that `rm -rf /` doesn't get run)

it did it! and, like, fuck. it did it *quite well* even. after the dust settled, i was pretty impressed... but also i felt entirely empty afterwards. why?

well, i wasn't doing this to meet a deadline. i have no stakeholders, i did not invest anything into this project, i had no intent to publish it let alone sell it, so what did i accomplish here? i told my computer to do something, and it did it. or rather, someone else's computer did, while mine acted as a dumb web proxy. maybe if these things did apply to me i would feel different, but when it comes to silly little impractical coding projects, i don't do these things to have a thing. there's often functional reasons for me taking on a project, but it's the satisfaction of doing that drives me to do it.

there's a lot of reasons to be morally opposed to claude & co., so i won't waste time here getting too into it. you already have your reasons, same as i, but the thing that really stung when i tried it? it just, felt unsatisfying. i had a new toy i could play with, this dev board that was suddenly pretty good at being a very slow usb drive, but i felt intensely conflicted about it. i certainly wouldn't feel comfortable uploading that "project" to github, under my own name, because *i didn't make it*. i certainly didn't learn anything, the mishmash of tinyusb initialization and USB MSC callbacks in the code made absolutely no sense to me in the aftermath than they would've before. in the end i decided the code wasn't even worth putting into my projects folder to be backed up automatically, lest i forget the source of it and out of sheer forgetfulness start claiming it was made by myself.

to me, the killer was treating my hobbies, my projects, like they were products to be marketed to the wider internet. that i wasn't there to solve problems and learn, but instead that i need to make a Cool Thing that everyone will ogle and go "ooh" and "aah" at. unfortunately, that is kind of what people make with these things, isn't it? hollow, uncreative minds prompting computers to make things that bleed of incuriosity rather than taking the time to learn the skills to do it themselves; pc ports of old n64 games without any of the prerequisite knowledge or passion required to reverse engineer it, porting one game's mechanics to another, things barely a level above what fortnite does by shoving franchise A next to franchise B. open source consumerism.

when has that ever described me? hell, i'm cagey about putting most of my code online, and i had to spend a lot of time convincing myself that it was worth migrating the handful of projects i did upload from bitbucket to github. the idea of being popular scares the shit out of me. the reason i publish anything is the vain hope that maybe someone will find it useful or interesting, and i guess also to pad my resume a little, right? why do i need to posture and pretend like telling claude to do my hobbies for me is a compelling thing for me as a developer to be doing? call me a luddite, but are you a developer anymore if you don't touch a line of code?

i'm willing to concede that the technology is impressive, and i'm sure with time it'll become the norm. i'll resist it until it becomes inevitable. there is certainly a case to be made in a popular oss project that maybe productivity does matter, and that there is a use case there... but i ask a question to the passionate AI evangelist who goes around bragging about their token usage, who seem more interested in having a thing they made than making that thing, who is eager to hop into my replies to call me a technophobe or reactionary:

you wouldn't add KPIs to your open source project, so why treat it like a job?
